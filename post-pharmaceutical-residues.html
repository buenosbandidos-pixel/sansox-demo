Pharmaceutical residues

The EU has confirmed that the pharmaceutical and cosmetics industry must help pay for removing micropollutants from wastewater. A pilot at the Salo treatment plant shows the removal itself already works.

A recent EU decision confirms that the pharmaceutical and cosmetics industry must contribute to the costs of removing micropollutants from wastewater. The decision is tied to the EU's new Wastewater Directive, which requires plants to remove substances that were never part of their original job — pharmaceutical residues among them.

According to Helsingin Sanomat, pharmaceuticals and cosmetics account for up to 92 % of the micropollutants in urban wastewater, and more than 1,400 kilograms of pharmaceuticals end up in Finnish waterways every year.

Alongside the directive the EU has introduced extended producer responsibility, built on the polluter-pays principle: the pharmaceutical and cosmetics industry must cover at least 80 % of the cost of removing micropollutants at treatment plants.

A Finnish pilot shows the removal works

The technology has already been tested in practice. SansOx ran a pilot at the City of Salo wastewater treatment plant together with Savonia University of Applied Sciences and the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL), treating the plant's discharge water with PharmOx.

The measured results:

  • Pharmaceutical removal efficiency up to 100 %
  • E. coli bacteria removed completely
  • A significant reduction in PFAS compounds
  • Dissolved oxygen in the water raised to over 21 mg/l

The same pass removes pharmaceutical residues and raises the oxygen content of the water, which supports the ecological status of the receiving water body.

Plants were built for nutrients, not for micropollutants

Existing wastewater plants are designed above all to remove nutrients — phosphorus and nitrogen. Micropollutant removal is a new requirement, and one the directive now makes unavoidable. Treating the discharge water is where SansOx sees the opening: the plant stops being only a source of load on the water body and becomes part of restoring it.

“The new EU regulation will significantly change wastewater treatment. At the same time it opens the door to technologies that both remove pharmaceutical residues and improve the oxygen situation in the receiving waters,” says Mikael Seppälä, CEO of SansOx Oy.

Polluter pays changes who can afford it

Because the directive puts the majority of the cost on the pharmaceutical and cosmetics industry, water utilities can adopt new solutions without carrying the investment alone.

“The directive changes the financing model. Water utilities no longer have to bear the costs by themselves — solutions can be implemented on the polluter-pays principle,” Seppälä states.

What happens next

The Salo pilot shows that micropollutant removal and oxygenation of the discharge water can be combined in one treatment step. The next step is to widen the pilots to new sites in Finland and in Europe.
